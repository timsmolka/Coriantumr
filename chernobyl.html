00 — On this record

What this is, and how it was kept honest

This reconstructs, as precisely as the record allows, the order of events from the start of the turbine-rundown safety test late on 25 April 1986, through the explosion at 01:23 on 26 April, the firefight and the denial-bound response, to roughly the same time the next night — the point at which the minute-by-minute record thins into an overview.

It is built only from widely acknowledged sources: the IAEA's INSAG-1 (1986) and its correction INSAG-7 (1992); UNSCEAR, WHO and IAEA casualty data; the World Nuclear Association and OECD-NEA technical sequences; and the standard histories by Medvedev, Dyatlov, Plokhy, Higginbotham and Alexievich.

  1. Every error-prone fact was independently cross-checked — timestamps, name spellings, roles, death dates. Where sources genuinely disagree, the disagreement is stated, not smoothed over.
  2. Documented testimony is separated from dramatization. Lines invented for the 2019 HBO series — "3.6 roentgen — not great, not terrible," the "Bridge of Death" — are kept out of the narrative and quarantined, labelled, in Appendix C.
  3. The gravest uncertainties are flagged inline — the exact second of the explosion, why AZ-5 was pressed, the cause of the power drop, a handful of death dates.
Sourcing caveat

Assembled largely from cross-checked search summaries of the sources above; direct full-text access to some primary hosts (the INSAG-7 PDF, IAEA, WNA) was not available during compilation. Well-established facts are corroborated across multiple independent sources; anything resting on a single source or in genuine dispute is marked as such.

Terms & abbreviations

The Soviet plant shorthand, in plain words

The abbreviations the record uses, spelled out — kept here on the side so the timeline can stay in the plant's own shorthand.

  • BShchU-4 — the Unit 4 main control room (Russian Blochny Shchit Upravleniya, "block/unit control panel"); the operators worked here, at +10.
  • AZ-5 — the emergency-shutdown ("full scram") button that drops every control rod (Russian Avariynaya Zashchita, "emergency protection," 5th level).
  • RBMK — the reactor type: a graphite-moderated, water-cooled, channel-type reactor (Reaktor Bolshoy Moshchnosti Kanalny, "high-power channel reactor").
  • ECCS — Emergency Core Cooling System: the emergency water supply meant to flood the core.
  • MCP — Main Circulation Pump (Russian GTsN): the big pumps driving coolant through the core; Khodemchuk's post.
  • SIUR / SIUB / SIUT — senior control engineers for the Reactor (SIUR — Toptunov), the unit / balance-of-plant (SIUB — Stolyarchuk) and the Turbine (SIUT).
  • ORM — Operating Reactivity Margin: the number of "equivalent" control rods left in the core; the safety floor was ~15, and it had sunk to ~6–8.
  • ChNPP — the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant itself.
  • MSCh-126 — Medical-Sanitary Unit No. 126, the Pripyat hospital that took the first casualties.
  • VPCh-2 / SVPCh-6 — the two paramilitary fire brigades: VPCh-2 based at the plant (Pravik), SVPCh-6 in Pripyat town (Kibenok).
  • VSRO / vent block — the ventilation-stack structure between Units 3 and 4, whose roof the firefighters climbed.
  • DP-5 — the military radiometer (200 R/h ceiling) that Vorobyov used off-site.
  • SKALA / DREG — the plant's central process computer (SKALA) and its event logger (DREG), which recorded the AZ-5 press (commonly cited at 01:23:40; some reconstructions ~01:23:39).
  • Deaerator — kit that strips dissolved gas from feed-water; its long "Golden Corridor" gallery is the plant's +10 spine.
  • Bubbler pool — the pressure-suppression water pool beneath the reactor (the "divers" drained it).
  • "Elena" (the pyatak) — the ~1,000-tonne upper biological-shield disc over the core, blown up and left tilted.
  • Corium — the lava-like melt of fuel, cladding, graphite and concrete.
  • Hydrogen (why it was in the turbine hall) — the big turbogenerators were hydrogen-cooled: hydrogen gas circulates inside the sealed generator casing as the coolant (it carries heat well and, being very light, causes little drag on the fast-spinning rotor), and the plant made its own on an on-site electrolysis station. Hydrogen is violently explosive mixed with air, so after the blast the electrical and turbine crews raced to shut off the supply and purge the generators with inert nitrogen — averting a second blast in a hall already full of burning oil, feet from the still-running Unit 3. (Separate from the reactor-side hydrogen made by the zirconium–steam reaction, a debated contributor to the second explosion — that one no one could switch off.)
  • Elevation marks (+NN) — metres above the +0.0 ground / transport level; control room +10, core top +35.5, roofs to ~+71.
  • R/h · rem · Gy · Sv — radiation units: R/h = roentgens per hour (a dose rate); rem and Sv measure absorbed dose to a person (1 Sv = 100 rem); Gy (gray) is absorbed energy. Fatal whole-body ARS begins around ~4–6 Gy/Sv.

The command knot — formal vs de-facto

By the book, the station shift supervisor Boris Rogozhkin (NSS — senior on-duty officer for the whole plant) sat above unit shift chief Akimov, and Unit 4 was Akimov's to run. But Dyatlov, a deputy chief engineer, outranked them both and was in the room directing the test, so real command ran Dyatlov → Akimov → Toptunov, with Rogozhkin sidelined. The test program never cleanly named one accountable director — which is why blame was so contestable (at trial Dyatlov even tried to pin the power reduction on Rogozhkin's orders).

Why the day shift matters here

The night crew were being carried out by dawn, so from ~06:00–08:00 the plant was run by replacements sent into a reactor no one in charge would admit was destroyed. Sitnikov, ordered up to look and then disbelieved, is the clearest case: he confirmed the truth with his own (fatal) eyes and was still overruled — the same denial that had governed the night, now killing the men brought in to relieve it.

Part I — Minute-by-minute chronicle

Where everyone was, and what they did

The reactor was to be shut for maintenance. Engineers used the shutdown to run a long-delayed safety test: could a coasting-down turbogenerator, spinning on its own inertia after a power cut, keep the cooling pumps running for the ~40–60 seconds until the diesels took over? On paper, an electrical test — run by a visiting specialist, Gennady Metlenko.

The day before · 25 April 1986

Reactor No. 4 had run since December 1983; on 25 April it was being taken down for routine annual maintenance, and the shutdown was the long-awaited chance to finally run the turbine-rundown test — one that had been attempted without success three times before (1982, 1984, 1985). The experienced day shift had prepared for it; a grid-dispatcher delay (below) would hand it instead to the night shift. In lighter detail than the night that follows:

~00:0025 Apr

Unit 4 is running at full power (~3200 MWt), feeding the grid; the day's scheduled shutdown will be used to run the test.

01:06

Deliberate power reduction of Unit 4 begins from full power — the first step of preparing for the turbogenerator No. 8 rundown test.

03:47

Power reduced to ~1600 MWt (about 50%); the reactor is held near half power for test preparation.

14:00

The Emergency Core Cooling System (ECCS) is isolated for the test. About the same time the Kiev grid dispatcher asks that further power reduction be postponed to cover the evening demand peak. The reactor is held at ~1600 MWt for roughly nine hours — long enough for the neutron poison xenon-135 to build up.

INSAG-7: the disabled ECCS did not materially affect the accident.

23:10

The dispatcher releases the unit; power reduction resumes toward the test band.

Correction to the popular story

The dispatcher postponed the power reduction, not the test. Its real effect was to push the experiment from the experienced day shift into the hands of the night shift, who had not prepared for it.

The shifts that inherited it

The experienced day shift — the crew who had studied the test program and expected to run it in daylight — spent the 25th preparing for an experiment the afternoon dispatcher hold then took out of their hands. Through the evening the reactor sat at half power under Yuri Tregub's evening shift, and it was Tregub's crew that began the final power reduction the moment the dispatcher released the unit at 23:10 — so the run-down was already under way when, at midnight, they handed a descending reactor to Aleksandr Akimov's night shift. Tregub, his own shift over, stayed on voluntarily to watch the unfamiliar test he had helped prepare. At the reactor console sat Leonid Toptunov — twenty-five, three years out of the Obninsk nuclear-engineering institute and barely two months in the senior reactor-control seat, working his first shutdown; Akimov, the conscientious shift chief who deferred to Dyatlov, was thirty-two. In the plainest terms it was a young, tired crew running an unfamiliar test in the small hours, on a reactor already sliding toward instability.

Why the personal record here is the thinnest of the night

The vivid control-room drama that fills popular retellings — Dyatlov screaming, Akimov pleading to abort, Toptunov refusing to raise power — comes largely from Grigori Medvedev's reconstructed dialogue, which survivors dispute and which INSAG-7's shift onto the reactor's hidden design flaws substantially undercuts. The two men who could have told it first-hand, Akimov and Toptunov, were dead within weeks, too ill to give structured testimony; the survivors who were in the room recall it as far quieter than the screen versions — Stolyarchuk has said flatly, "there were no serious discussions between the staff that night." So for these hours the honest record preserves their movements and their inexperience, not their words — and that restraint is deliberate.

The 00:28 power drop — corrected

For years this was blamed on operator error by Toptunov. INSAG-7 (1992) substantially walked that back, attributing the accident primarily to the reactor's design rather than the operators; later summaries (the World Nuclear Association, following the Soviet SCSSINP commission and Dyatlov) put it more bluntly as "no operator error as such" and an unknown cause. (That blunt phrasing is WNA's, not a verbatim INSAG-7 line.) Toptunov was at the console, but the drop is not attributed to his fault.

The 01:22:30 "printout" — disputed. The traditional account has the crew receiving a SKALA printout warning of the low margin. The WNA disputes this: SKALA needed ~10–15 minutes to compute it and had not done so, so the crew probably had no live reading. The low margin is not in doubt; whether anyone saw it in time is.

01:23:04

Control room The test begins. On Akimov's order Kirschenbaum closes the No. 8 turbine stop valves; Metlenko calls "oscillograph on — start." Why a "shutdown" raised power

The RBMK was over-moderated by its graphite stack, so the cooling water in the channels acted mainly as a neutron absorber — the same reason the reactor had a positive void coefficient (losing water raised power). The rods were not simply "tipped" with graphite: each carried a separate graphite displacer (a "follower") — a graphite column hung below the boron-carbide absorber and shorter than the channel, so that with the rod fully withdrawn the graphite, not water, filled the core region, with short columns of water above and below it. Pressing AZ-5 (on the near-zero margin) drove that displacer down, and its lower end pushed the neutron-absorbing water out of the bottom of the channel first, putting non-absorbing, moderating graphite in its place before the boron absorber could reach that region — injecting a burst of positive reactivity at the base of the core and turning the shutdown into the trigger of the excursion. ("Graphite-tipped" is the usual shorthand for that displacer design.) This was not new: the RBMK's positive-feedback instability had shown itself at the Leningrad-1 RBMK in 1975, and the specific graphite-tip scram effect was identified in RBMK commissioning tests around 1983 — flagged to the designers but never passed on to the operators, who believed AZ-5 was a pure fail-safe. The flaw was corrected on all RBMKs afterward. (The reactor type's fragility had also been shown at Chernobyl itself — a covered-up partial fuel-channel meltdown of Unit 1 on 9 September 1982, told in full in "The full story." That the defect was known and concealed is a large part of why INSAG-7 shifted the blame off the operators — see Appendix C.)

The exact second — genuinely disputed

All final-second times are simulation reconstructions; the instruments were destroyed. The most-cited value is 01:23:45 (WNA: "at 01:23:45 the 1000 t lid was lifted"). Others give :44, :47 or :48. Seismic records show two Rayleigh waves ~2.7 s apart. The nature of the second blast (hydrogen and/or a further excursion) remains debated.

The press is commonly cited at 01:23:40; some DREG-based reconstructions read ~01:23:39 (both are logger-derived — DREG runs on SKALA — so a clean "DREG vs SKALA" split isn't firmly supported). The control room · BShchU-4

Anatoly Stepanovich Dyatlov

Deputy Chief Engineer for Operations — the senior man present, supervising the test · age 55

Widely described as strict, domineering, and abrasive, but respected for deep reactor knowledge. He pushed the test forward at the unstable 200 MWt. After the blast he fell into adamant denial: convinced a hydrogen build-up had blown the emergency-feedwater (deaerator) tank — not the reactor — his first fear was scalding water raining from the deaerators above the control room — so he briefly ordered the crew out to the reserve panel, then rescinded it when no scalding rain came, and kept them at their posts. When the reactor-shop supervisor came in to say the reactor itself was destroyed, he refused to believe him. Why the denial held: the dosimeters were dead or off-scale, the instruments blank, and the total destruction of an RBMK core was, to him, almost literally unthinkable.

He told Akimov, "Lads, we've got to get water into the reactor," and sent trainees Proskuryakov and Kudryavtsev up to hand-crank the rods down — the one order he later called his "only mistaken command" (rods that won't fall by gravity can't be moved by hand). He walked the wrecked exterior with Tregub — Tregub: "This is Hiroshima." Dyatlov: "Not in my nightmares have I seen anything like this." Then, beginning to vomit, he gathered the operating logs and left for the admin block to report. In his memoir he blamed the RBMK's design, not his operators — a position later substantially vindicated by INSAG-7.

"The time has come to say openly: the design of the RBMK-1000 was … the sole reason for the Chernobyl accident." Documented — Dyatlov, Chernobyl: How It Was (1995)

Survived (~3.9 Sv) · convicted July 1987, 10 years, released 1990 · married (wife Isabella, a Pripyat kindergarten teacher); one of their sons had died of leukaemia in childhood before the disaster · died 1995, heart failure linked to his exposure.

Aleksandr Fyodorovich Akimov

Shift supervisor of the Unit 4 night shift — in charge of the crew during the test · age 32 (had just turned 33 on 6 May, four days before his death on 10 May)

His demeanor moved from disbelief to anguished, self-sacrificing duty — Stolyarchuk remembered him "very pale." In the first minutes he ordered the emergency-cooling pumps started and had Perevozchenko open the system valves; ordered Stolyarchuk and Busygin to switch on the emergency feed-water pumps; and, on Dyatlov's instruction, phoned the fire brigade and the day shift. From the falsely low readings he concluded the reactor was intact, and reported that up to Bryukhanov at ~02:30.

Then, still believing water could save the core, he stayed on past his relief and — with Toptunov, Nekhaev, Orlov and Uskov — went up from the control room (+10) into the wrecked feed-water room at +24, opening the two feedwater-line valves, then climbed to the +27 gallery (Room 712) and opened the "300 line" by hand, knee-deep in a fuel-and-water mixture, for about half an hour — taking an estimated 15–20 Sv. A colleague who saw him in Moscow described him swollen and turned "deep reddish-brown," consumed by bewilderment and guilt.

"We did everything correctly … Why?" Reported — via Medvedev / Smagin, spoken while dying

Died of ARS, 10 May 1986 (some sources 11 May), Hospital No. 6.

Leonid Fyodorovich Toptunov

Senior Reactor Control Engineer (SIUR) — operating the rods at the console · age 25

Three years out of the Obninsk nuclear-engineering institute (he joined the plant in March 1983) and only about two months in the senior reactor-control seat, working his first reactor shutdown at the console; young and visibly aware of it, under Dyatlov's pressure. He was at the controls for the power drop and its recovery, and physically pressed the AZ-5 button on Akimov's order — Stolyarchuk remembered him afterward "literally white." Dyatlov ordered him (with Kirschenbaum) out to the Unit 3 control room as "of no further use," but Toptunov went back into the wrecked room to retrieve the operator's log and stayed on duty, then joined Akimov's futile valve-opening effort in lethal radiation (~13 Sv).

Disputed

The often-told story that Toptunov "refused" to raise power is disputed — no control-room survivor confirms such an argument.

Died of ARS, 14 May 1986, despite a bone-marrow transplant. Buried at Mitino.

Why these two can't be taken deeper

Akimov and Toptunov are the pivotal operators of the night — yet, unlike the survivors, their inner experience cannot be reconstructed first-hand. Both died within weeks, too ill to give structured testimony, so everything past their documented movements (the panel, the AZ-5 press, the valves, the water) is second-hand recollection. The people who can carry a Yuvchenko-style minute-by-minute are the ones who lived to tell it — Dyatlov, Stolyarchuk, Davletbayev, Tregub, Genrikh, Gorbachenko, Belokon. For the dead we record what they did, not what they felt. That limit is deliberate.

Boris Vladimirovich Stolyarchuk

Senior Unit Control Engineer (feedwater/turbine systems) — one of very few in the room to survive

At his desk the readings showed all eight main safety valves open, yet no water left in the separators. Akimov ordered him — with Busygin — to switch on the emergency feed-water pumps; he was one of only two men (with Akimov) not dismissed to Unit 3, and he stayed at the panel for roughly three hours, lining up and holding pumps by hand. Why he lived: he spent the night at +10, shielded by the massive concrete deaerator stack — the single fact that most explains why he survived while men one plane of exposure away did not. Calm and matter-of-fact in later recollection, he survived his dose and has given many interviews, firmly blaming the reactor's design and saying the HBO series exaggerated the panic: "There were no serious discussions between the staff that night."

"The walls began to shake and the concrete itself made a kind of creaking noise. That's when I realised something terrible had happened." Documented — Boris Stolyarchuk, interview

Survived. One of the last living eyewitnesses of the control room.

Igor Kirschenbaum (also Kershenbaum)

Senior Turbine Control Engineer

On Akimov's instruction he closed the turbine stop valves at 01:23:04 to start the test. Present through the explosion.

Survived.

Yuri Eduardovich Tregub

Shift foreman of the preceding shift, who stayed on to observe

His own shift had ended, but he stayed on as a volunteer to watch the test he had helped prepare — which is why Dyatlov used him as a runner afterward. Quick-reacting under shock, he was sent from the control room to open the emergency-coolant (ECCS) valves by hand; needing a second man, he was joined by Yuvchenko. The two went down two flights into knee-deep water to a jammed door, glimpsed the tanks "torn apart like wet cardboard," were driven back, and turned out through the ground-level (+0.0) transport corridor into the open ~50 m from the reactor — where they saw the blue beam of an unshielded core and pulled back around the corner. Back inside, in the +10 corridor, their paths ←→ splitsplit: Yuvchenko went on (with Perevozchenko and the trainees) to hold the reactor-hall door, while Tregub carried their report on to the control room — where Akimov and Dyatlov would not accept it: Dyatlov held that an emergency feed-water tank had burst and the reactor was whole, and the standing order to keep pumping water never changed. He later walked the wrecked exterior with Dyatlov. (His documented emergency-valve attempt is the joint one with Yuvchenko; a further solo valve-route is not in the record.) (A separate, contested account has Tregub make the valve attempt with operator Gazin instead — the two tellings can't be cleanly reconciled.)

"This is Hiroshima." Reported — attributed to Tregub via Medvedev, whose account is treated with caution

Survived (hospitalised).

The reactor hall, central hall & pump hall

Valery Ilyich Khodemchuk

Senior main circulation pump operator

At his post beside the reactor when it exploded; killed instantly — the first person to die. His body was never recovered and lies entombed in the ruins; a plaque marks the spot inside the plant. He is the man his colleagues searched for in vain. No testimony of his survives.

Killed ~01:23, 26 April 1986. No grave.

Alexander Petrovich Yuvchenko

Senior mechanical engineer, reactor section · age 24 — one of the few in the reactor building to survive

At the blast he was upstairs in the windowless senior-engineers' room on level +12.5, between Units 3 and 4. He felt "a thud, a shaking," then — two or three seconds later — the explosion: "a wave came through the room; the thick concrete walls bent like rubber. I thought war had broken out." The doors blew in and a choking milky-grey dust swept through. He groped along his desk for the phone to Control Room 4 — the line was dead; but Control Room 3 (Unit 3) rang through with an order to bring stretchers.

He grabbed a stretcher and started down toward level +10 to carry out the wounded, where a dazed figure, clothing blackened and face bloody and unrecognisable, stopped him — he knew it was his friend, pump operator Viktor Degtyarenko, only by his voice. Told others were hurt near the coolant tanks, he took a torch and searched the wreckage, finding a second scalded operator who had crawled clear — soaked, filthy and shaking, but still on his feet; another waved him off: "I'm all right. Help Khodemchuk — he's in the pump room." documented

Was the story that Yuvchenko "carried someone" real? It is rooted in fact but slightly overstated: he did take up a stretcher and go to the wounded — but in his own account the two scalded men he reached were still on their feet, and he was pulled straight on to the coolant valves with Tregub, so the record does not have him carrying any one man to safety.

How he came to be outside. Back in the +10 corridor near the control room he ran into Tregub, whom Akimov had sent from the control room to open the emergency-coolant (ECCS) valves by hand; the job needed two men, so the strongly-built Yuvchenko went with him. The nearest way in was blocked by rubble, so they went down two flights of stairs — and were at once knee-deep in water at a jammed hall door, through the gap the great tanks "torn apart like wet cardboard," stars showing where the ceiling had been. Driven back, they turned into the ground-level (+0.0) transport corridor and stepped outside, about 50 m from the reactor, where Yuvchenko saw the pillar of light — until, recognising an open, unshielded core, they pulled back around the corner. (Which stairwell they took down, the water's exact level, and the door they exited are not in the record — only "two flights down" and "out through the transport corridor.") reported route

"From where I stood I could see a huge beam of projected light flooding up into infinity from the reactor. It was like a laser light … it was light-bluish, and it was very beautiful." Documented — New Scientist, 2004

Then the door. Running back to report, he and Tregub were stopped in the +10 corridor by Perevozchenko and the trainees Proskuryakov and Kudryavtsev, whom Dyatlov had ordered up to lower the control rods by hand. Yuvchenko told them the mission was senseless — the control rods, and the reactor itself, no longer existed — but Perevozchenko countered that Yuvchenko had only seen it "from below," and they needed to assess the damage from above. Orders were orders, and Yuvchenko had the only flashlight, so he agreed to help find the way. Tregub went on to the control room; Yuvchenko joined the other three, and they climbed the stairs from ~+12 up toward +35 (Yuvchenko last, "through a labyrinth of collapsed walls") to the airlock door. Almost certainly this was the vent-block (VSRO) stairwell the trainees had just used, though the source does not name it. "Because I had come up the stairs last, I stayed behind propping open the door. They took the torch from me and went in." He braced his shoulder against the heavy steel door for "a minute at most" while the three stepped onto the ledge over the crater. Then, as they staggered back into the corridor in shock, Yuvchenko wanted to look into the core for himself — but Perevozchenko, a veteran of the nuclear-submarine fleet who understood exactly what was there, shoved the younger man aside and the door slammed shut: "There's nothing to see here." That shove almost certainly saved him; the men who had looked in were fatally dosed in seconds. His own burns were to his left shoulder, hip and calf, the parts pressed against the radioactive door — which is why, as much as the shove, he credited the shielding: "That wall and the door basically saved my life."

He began vomiting around 3 a.m. and could not walk by ~6 a.m.; he was taken to Pripyat's MSCh-126 and flown to Moscow that evening. Along the way he passed a man whose dosimeter needle was pinned off the scale — which is why his own dose (commonly given as ~4.1 Sv) is a later clinical estimate, not a measurement.

Correcting the popular version

He was not "thrown to the floor" — he stayed on his feet; and he held the door because he came up last, not because he was the strongest (the "champion athlete" description belongs to firefighter Vasily Ignatenko). It was Perevozchenko, not Yuvchenko, who went on into the wrecked pump hall (+10) searching for Khodemchuk — the board-crawl detail is a soft one (see his entry). Where Yuvchenko was: his own documented posts were the central-hall airlock door up at +35.5 and then the +10 corridor with the brief joint search; when Perevozchenko pressed on alone into the pump hall, Yuvchenko stayed back and did not follow. The exact spot he waited is not recorded.

Survived — almost alone among the reactor-building men of that night. About a year in Hospital No. 6, kept alive by continuous transfusions ("I lived on other people's blood") through many skin grafts as the radiation ulcers opened · married, with a young son, Kirill · became a widely-interviewed witness · died of leukemia in 2008, aged 47.

Valery Ivanovich Perevozchenko

Reactor-section shift foreman; a former nuclear-submarine sailor

Desperate and self-sacrificing. He led Proskuryakov and Kudryavtsev toward the reactor hall (Yuvchenko holding the door) and looked into the open core himself — burns later mapped to his side and back. He carried word back to the control room that the reactor itself was destroyed — and Akimov, still certain it was intact, told him it was not true. After the door he stayed with Yuvchenko and, joined by Dyatlov, searched the corridors for the missing Khodemchuk with the low-range dosimeters pegged at maximum. When that failed and the others peeled away, he pressed on alone through graphite- and fuel-strewn rubble on level +10 to the ruins of room 306, close to debris throwing >10,000 R/hr. He found nothing — Khodemchuk was already dead and entombed. By the time he reached the dosimetry room his face was already "tanned" by the radiation. He received a fatal dose.

Disputed / soft detail

Medvedev's dramatic scene of Perevozchenko watching the fuel-channel caps "dancing" before the blast is disputed — Yuvchenko stated he could not have been there. What it would have looked like is not itself in doubt: the reactor's top face at +35.5 is paved with the ~350 kg caps that seal each of the ~1,660 fuel channels, and as those channels overpressured and ruptured, the caps would have been heaved and rattled in their sockets in a ripple across the whole floor in the final second or two before the shield lifted. But that violent motion and the explosion were essentially one event, seconds apart — not a slow "dance" a man could stand in the hall and calmly watch, which is the part in dispute. The often-repeated image of him crawling a plank over a flooded cavity during the search is not in the source books — it appears to be a screen (miniseries) device, not a documented route detail, so it is not treated as fact here.

Died of ARS, 13 June 1986.

Viktor Proskuryakov & Aleksandr Kudryavtsev

Reactor control engineers / trainees, present to observe the test

Dyatlov sent them up to lower the control rods by hand — already impossible, the reactor being destroyed. They ran through the de-aerator gallery to the vent-block lift, found it wrecked, and climbed the stairs to level +36. In the hall they saw rubble, fire hoses pouring water into the wreck, the 1,000-tonne "Elena" shield jammed at an angle over the shaft, and blue and red flames in the reactor. Proskuryakov shone a flashlight around the corner into the hall — severe burns later appeared on that hand. They stayed only as long as Yuvchenko held the door — a minute at most; Proskuryakov returned to the control room and said he had looked into the destroyed core — which Dyatlov waved away as an exploded emergency feed-water tank, sending them back to keep trying to lower rods that no longer existed — then vomited and was taken to the infirmary.

Kudryavtsev died 14 May 1986; Proskuryakov died 17 May 1986.

Anatoly Kharlampievich Kurguz

Senior operator of the central reactor hall

He was in the +36 operators' room with three doors open toward the central hall, so he took the full force of the steam. A former submariner, he looked into the hall, saw a curtain of hot steam and dust, and heaved the heavy pressurised door shut as the steam flooded in — shielding Genrikh — then led others out from the upper floors before losing consciousness. Scalded across face and body; evacuated by ambulance.

Died of ARS / burns, 12 May 1986.

Oleg Genrikh

Reactor-hall operator, with Kurguz

Napping in a windowless room next to the operators' station, he was shielded from the steam and far less badly burned than Kurguz. The right-hand stairs were wrecked, so he escaped down the left-hand stairs — clear-headed enough to shower and change his clothes to reduce contamination before evacuating.

Survived; gave filmed interviews decades later.

Viktor Degtyarenko

Reactor (coolant pump) operator

Badly scalded by steam near his station. As Yuvchenko came down toward level +10 with a stretcher, a dazed figure with blackened clothes and a bloody, unrecognisable face stopped him — Yuvchenko knew it was Degtyarenko only by his voice; he said others near his station still needed help. Contrary to the impression left by the miniseries, he lived about a month.

What the reports got — a wall of denial

The reason so many accounts "just go back to report" and seem to stop is that the reports changed nothing. Through the night the control room heard the same message from man after man — Perevozchenko, the trainees Proskuryakov and Kudryavtsev, and Yuvchenko and Tregub all came back saying the reactor hall was gone and the core lay open — and each was refused. Akimov held that the reactor was intact; Dyatlov insisted the wreckage and the blue glow were an exploded emergency feed-water tank, and the standing order — keep feeding water to the core — never changed. So "after they reported," the men were sent back into the radiation: the trainees to keep lowering rods, Tregub and Yuvchenko to the coolant valves, Perevozchenko to search for Khodemchuk — while the water kept pouring into a reactor that no longer existed. The denial outlived the night: at ~10:00 deputy chief engineer Sitnikov climbed the block roof, saw the open core with his own (fatal) eyes and reported it to Fomin and Bryukhanov — and was still only partly believed.

The turbine (machine) hall

Razim Ilgamovich Davletbayev

Deputy chief of the turbine shop

Calm, methodical, duty-bound. He heard first a "rolling thunder, as during water hammers" and the building shudder, then the low roar; running into the turbine hall with Akimov, Brazhnik and Palamarchuk he found fires on levels 0 and +12, ruptured oil and water pipes, roof slabs fallen onto turbine No. 7, burning linoleum, and scattered graphite and fuel — a scene he called "worthy of the pen of the great Dante" — and reported loss of power, torn cables and electric arcs. Why they ran toward it: the machine hall held hydrogen-cooled generators and tonnes of turbine oil; had the fire reached them, a hydrogen blast could have spread down the shared roof to the still-running Unit 3 — a second catastrophe. Through the night he organised the crew to drain the oil, displace the generators' hydrogen with nitrogen, and beat down the oil fires — methodical work while his own men were dying around him. His memoir The Last Shift is the single best primary source for the turbine side.

"A roar of a completely unfamiliar kind, very low in tone, like a human moan." Documented — Davletbayev, The Last Shift

Survived the acute phase.

The turbine-hall crew

Machinists, mechanics and operators who fought fire and flood in the machine hall

Vyacheslav Brazhnik ran to the control room to report the fire, then shut off the turbine-oil pipeline to stop the blaze spreading — a fuel fragment lodged in a nearby transformer added to his fatal dose (✝ 14 May 1986). Anatoly Baranov, an electrical duty engineer, switched turbogenerators 3 and 4 from hydrogen to nitrogen cooling, preventing a hydrogen explosion (✝ 20 May 1986). Kostyantyn Perchuk cut the pumps off from fractured pipes to stop radioactive water flooding in from the deaerators, and fought the oil fire with Brazhnik (✝ 20 May 1986). Yuri Vershinin and Oleksandr Novik fought the engine-room fire — both died of ARS (Vershinin ✝ 21 July, Novik ✝ 26 July, per liquidator records). Arkady Uskov, Nekhaev and Viacheslav Orlov — day-shift engineers called in before dawn — waded knee-deep into the flooded +27 level to open feedwater valves alongside Akimov and Toptunov; all three survived the acute phase. Yuri Korneyev, at the turbines at the moment of the accident, survived — later the last living member of the turbine night shift, his radiation cataracts corrected years afterward.

Mixed — several dead ✝, several survived.

The electrical shop

Aleksandr Grigoryevich Lelechenko

Deputy chief of the electrical shop

He spotted an intense flow of hydrogen toward Units 3 and 4 that could only be shut off at the electrolysis plant. He ordered his younger crew to stand down and went himself — kneeling in radioactive water, stepping over graphite, and closing the heavy valves by hand, three times over — to cut the hydrogen and avert an explosion, then tried to restore power to the water pumps. His dose is estimated at ~2,500 R reported. After first aid at the Pripyat clinic he went back to work — reportedly even home for dinner and a few hours' sleep, then back again — continuing until ~30 April. With Viktor Lopatyuk (✝ 17 May) and Oleksandr Novik (✝ 26 July) he localised the fire to Unit 4.

Died of ARS, 7 May 1986 — one of the earliest deaths. Posthumously Hero of Ukraine.

Dosimetry & rescue

Nikolai Gorbachenko

Senior control-room dosimetrist

Horror, then duty. His instruments were useless — the one high-range meter was buried/failed on start-up, and his portable devices maxed at ~3.6–4 R/h while the true fields were thousands. He met Palamarchuk on the level +27 staircase; together they found and carried out the dying Shashenok, and Gorbachenko was left with a burn on his back exactly where Shashenok's hand had rested.

"Just as in a horror film, the blast blew out the double doors … Black-red dust started coming out of the ventilation vent." Documented — Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists

Survived; released later in 1986.

Serafim Vorobyov

Chief of the plant's civil-defense staff — the man who tried to raise the alarm

With the operators' own dosimeters dead or floored — Gorbachenko's high-range meter burned out when switched on, the portable ones pegged at ~4 R/h — the one instrument that could actually read the disaster was the military DP-5 radiometer (ceiling 200 R/h). Vorobyov took it through the plant grounds and the approaches to Pripyat and found the needle slamming to the top of the scale — off the 200 R/h ceiling — near the wrecked block. He carried the figure to Bryukhanov and urged that the town be warned to close its windows and stay inside; the director stalled, waited for a "second opinion," and treated the reading as a broken instrument. By his own later account he was pushed out for insisting — but he had the fields essentially right, hours before anyone acted.

This documented thread — the burned-out high-range meter, the DP-5 pegged at 200 R/h, and the dismissal — is what the 2019 series compresses into the single "Sitnikov with dosimeters" scene; see Appendix C.

Survived.

Vladimir Nikolaevich Shashenok

Automation / commissioning adjuster (Atomenergonaladka), in room 604

He was at his post in room 604 phoning pressure-gauge readings to the computer room. The pressure spike burst the isolation membranes and impulse lines of his manometers, then the room itself collapsed: he was found pinned under a fallen beam — broken spine and ribs, burns over most of his body, bloody foam at his mouth, his body severely contaminated by boiling radioactive water. Carried out unconscious by Gorbachenko and Palamarchuk, he died about 06:00 under chief physician Vitaly Leonenko, without regaining consciousness.

Died ~06:00, 26 April 1986 — the second fatality, and the first to be buried.

The firefighters

The first alarm was raised within minutes. The men came in ordinary gear, with no respirators, no working dosimeters, and no knowledge that the debris was intensely radioactive — they treated it as a conventional fire. Some kicked or picked up chunks of graphite, not knowing what it was (documented via driver Grigori Khmel in Alexievich). At its peak the response reached ~37 crews, ~186 firefighters, ~81 engines; the last surface fires were out by 06:35, leaving the graphite fire burning in the crater.

How they got up. Pravik parked at the ground-level (+0.0) transport corridor, found a phone to sound the "No. 3" alarm, and put men on the turbine-hall roof; Kibenok's trucks, arriving on the south side, were redeployed on Pravik's order to the north face ("Row B"), where an auto-ladder was set under the vent (VSRO) block to reach the external fire-escape. From there Pravik led four men up an external stairway on the north face to the Unit 3 roof (~01:56) and on to the ventilation-block roof (~+71 m); Kibenok climbed the VSRO roof ~01:42 with Ivanchenko and Romashevsky. (The much-repeated "~70 m northern fire-escape" is not firmly documented as the exact ascent — sources say only "up the ladder / to the roof.") reported route

Lt. Vladimir Pravik

Guard commander, plant brigade VPCh-2 — first on scene (~01:28) · age 23

Calm and decisive. First on scene at the north side of the block (~01:28), he raised the highest "No. 3" alarm, mobilising the whole Kiev oblast, and put his men onto the turbine-hall and reactor-block roofs. By ~02:10 he was already symptomatic — he handed roof command to Kibenok and went down to call for ambulances and relief. In hospital, during the deceptive latent phase of ARS, he wrote his wife an optimistic letter expecting to recover.

Died of ARS, 11 May 1986. He left a wife, Nadezhda, and a baby daughter (Natalya) born just before the disaster · Posthumous Hero of the Soviet Union; buried at Mitino.

Lt. Viktor Kibenok & his SVPCh-6 squad

Pripyat city brigade — first reinforcement (~01:35)

His nine-man guard arrived ~01:35 in three trucks, first at the turbine hall, then redeployed to the ventilation-block roof on Pravik's radio order. On the roof of Unit 3 the burning bitumen melted and stuck to their boots, its black smoke searing their airways; after about 20 minutes Tishura collapsed, then Titenok. Of Vasily Ignatenko, his wife recalled: "They kicked at the burning graphite with their feet… they weren't wearing their canvas gear, they went off just as they were, in their shirt sleeves." Kibenok told the ambulance doctor there were no burn casualties but the situation was unclear — then dropped to his knees vomiting. The squad: Kibenok (✝ 11 May), Vasily Ignatenko (✝ 13 May), Nikolai Vashchuk (✝ 14 May), Mykola Tytenok (✝ 16 May), and Vladimir Tishura — the first firefighter to die (✝ 10 May).

All five of this account ✝ died of ARS at Hospital No. 6, buried at Mitino in soldered zinc coffins.

Major Leonid Telyatnikov

Chief of VPCh-2 — off-duty, summoned from home; arrived ~01:40 and took overall command

Composed and authoritative. Alerted by phone, he reached the site about 10 minutes after the first crews; at ~02:30 he stopped in the Unit 4 control room, was told the priority was the Unit 3 and ventilation-block roofs, and sent three men up to relieve the first group. He climbed the Unit 3 roof himself amid at least five separate fires, ascending more than once, unaware of the radiation. By ~03:30 he was retching and was evacuated to the Pripyat hospital; that July, recovering, he was allowed to walk the ward in a gauze mask.

"The most frightening thought we all had was that we wouldn't have enough strength to hold out until reserves came." Documented — Telyatnikov, interview

Received ~4 Sv · survived the acute phase · Hero of the Soviet Union · died of cancer in 2004. He is sometimes wrongly listed among the 1986 dead; he was not.

The surviving firefighters

The men who came, were dosed, and lived

The Shavrey brothers — Leonid, Ivan and Petr, from Belarus — all fought the fire and all lived. Ivan worked the roof at ~36 m for about 20 minutes until he fainted; Leonid, who took the highest dose of any Chernobyl firefighter, was saved by treatment in Kyiv under Dr. Leonid Kindzelsky (he lived until 2012; Ivan died in 2020 of COVID-19). Driver Grigori Khmel arrived from Chernobyl town — "Is that graphite?" someone asked; he kicked a piece aside, and a man on another truck picked one up: "It's hot." Lt. Piotr Khmel, off-duty and drinking when the call came, grabbed the half-empty bottle of Soviet champagne off his table and drained it in the jeep on the way. Relief-squad men Alexander Petrovsky and Anatoly Ivanchenko also survived; so did Anatoly Zakharov, who understood the danger and went anyway.

"Of course we knew! If we'd followed regulations, we would never have gone near the reactor. But it was a moral obligation — our duty. We were like kamikaze." Documented — Anatoly Zakharov, 2008 interview

Survived.

Dr. Valentin Belokon

Pripyat ambulance physician on duty

Professional and alert. The first ambulance out — Dr. Skachek with driver Gusarov, in no protective clothing — left within minutes; Belokon followed with more, and the first casualties reached the Pripyat clinic about 40 minutes after the blast. Expecting burns, he instead found nausea, splitting headaches, dry and blocked throats, men disoriented and losing speech — an 18-year-old brought to him vomiting again and again. Mid-conversation, Kibenok collapsed vomiting at his feet. Back in Pripyat before 10 a.m. he made the diagnosis: radiation sickness. He was himself hospitalised for months.

Survived (hospitalised for months).

"A solitary human voice" — Lyudmila Ignatenko

Lyudmila Ignatenko

Wife of firefighter Vasily Ignatenko; pregnant at the time — the central primary testimony of the disaster

Love shading into grief and melancholy, and fierce defiance. About six months pregnant, she concealed it — telling the staff she had no children — to stay at Vasily's bedside in Hospital No. 6 through his disintegration, against the doctors' warnings.

"You have to understand: this is not your husband anymore, not a beloved person, but a radioactive object with a strong density of poisoning… That's not a person anymore, that's a nuclear reactor." Documented (as testimony) — hospital staff to Lyudmila, in Alexievich

Two honest caveats

Her words are authentically documented oral history. But the claim within it that the baby "absorbed" the radiation and shielded her is medically disputed — an externally irradiated person does not become a radioactive source to others. And Alexievich's book is literary oral history, shaped and compressed, not a clinical transcript.

Survived; her daughter, born after Vasily's death, lived only hours.

Part III — The rest of the first day · 26 April into the small hours of the 27th

A tragedy of denial — dawn on the 26th to ~01:23 on the 27th

The night hours are in Part I. The men who confirmed, and denied, the truth

Anatoly Sitnikov

Deputy chief engineer, Units 1–2 — a reactor physicist

Dutiful and self-sacrificing. Ordered by Fomin (with Bryukhanov's backing) to settle the argument management refused to accept, he climbed high — accounts place him on the Unit 3 / block roof, looking down into the wrecked central hall and the crater — and confirmed with his own eyes that the core was destroyed and open. Why him: he was a physicist and deputy chief engineer, senior enough that his word could not be waved away. He absorbed a massive dose (some accounts ~15 Sv, heavy to the head), came down visibly burned, delivered the truth — and was still partly disbelieved. (The 2019 series folds his disbelieved report together with Vorobyov's dismissed ~200 R/h reading into a single "Sitnikov with dosimeters" scene, and adds an armed-guard march to the roof that did not happen — see Appendix C.)

Died of ARS, 30 May 1986.

Viktor Bryukhanov

Plant director

Calm denial verging on psychological shock. When civil-defense chief Serafim Vorobyov, whose radiometer pegged its 200 R/h ceiling, urged that the town be warned, Bryukhanov stalled and waited for a second dosimetrist rather than act (the crisp "your instrument is broken" rebuke is dramatized); at ~03:00 he phoned Moscow that the situation was under control. He later admitted: "I looked — pieces of graphite under my feet. But I still did not think that the reactor was destroyed."

Survived · convicted July 1987, 10 years, released ~1991 · died 2021 in Kyiv.

Nikolai Fomin

Chief engineer

Bore management responsibility for approving the test. That night he ordered continuous water into the reactor and fed replacement men into Unit 4 (the "hysterical / near-breakdown" close-ups are dramatized; the actions are documented). Under prosecution came a real collapse: before trial he attempted suicide in his cell, and later suffered a nervous breakdown and psychiatric confinement.

Survived · convicted · released; later worked at the Kalinin plant — death not reliably recorded (the common "2011" is unsupported).

Valery Legasov

First Deputy Director, Kurchatov Institute — chief scientist of the Commission

On the flight in he recalled a huge crimson glow over half the visible sky. Driven and physically committed. As Shcherbina's lead scientist he shaped the technical response: he pressed hard — and successfully — for the immediate evacuation of Pripyat; he chose the materials dropped on the open core (boron carbide as a neutron absorber, lead to shield and absorb heat, sand, clay and dolomite to blanket the fire); and he was central to the alarm over the bubbler pool and to the foundation-cooling effort — the divers and the miners. He spent much of the next four months on or near the site, taking a heavy dose.

In August 1986 he led the Soviet delegation to the IAEA in Vienna and delivered a marathon ~5-hour report — unusually candid about the sequence and scale, which calmed the international community but, because it still pinned the cause on operator error and spared the RBMK's designers, left him resented at home. Passed over for honours and increasingly despairing at the refusal to confront the reactor's flaws, he dictated ~five cassette tapes of memoirs.

"It didn't even occur to me … that we were moving towards an event on a planetary scale." Documented — Legasov's tapes

Absorbed heavy radiation · took his own life on 27 April 1988 — two years and one day after the accident. The RBMK design fixes he had urged were quietly made across the fleet; in 1996 he was named Hero of the Russian Federation, posthumously.

Meanwhile, in Pripyat · 26 April

While the plant burned, the town of ~49,000 — a young company town, average age ~26 — lived a normal warm Saturday, unwarned. Children played outside; mothers pushed strollers; people gardened at their dachas and fished in the plant's warm outflow channel (Medvedev names anglers Pustovoit and Protasov, who received very high doses). It was a normal Soviet school Saturday — morning lessons and open-air exercises on the school squares. Several weddings were held (a commonly cited figure is four); the town band Pulsar played. The new stadium and the amusement park with its Ferris wheel were being readied for a May Day opening that never came — the oft-repeated story that the fair was opened early on the 27th to calm residents is disputed. A neighbour sunbathed, marvelling at how easily he tanned, and was hospitalised that evening. As invisible fallout settled, some residents gathered on balconies; Alexievich records one lifting children to admire the coloured glow, standing in black dust. Radiation ran into the tens of milliroentgens per hour and rising; iodine prophylaxis, when it came, was late and largely futile.

The general who drove in himself — Col.-Gen. Vladimir Pikalov

Commander of the Soviet Chemical Troops; a three-star general. Cool, decisive, and unwilling to spend a subordinate's life on a run he would not make himself. Vladimir Karpovich Pikalov (b. 15 September 1924, Armavir) was a decorated artilleryman of the Second World War — he had fought at Moscow, Stalingrad and Kursk and been wounded more than once — who retrained afterward as a military chemist and had led the Chemical Troops for nearly two decades. He reached Chernobyl on the afternoon of 26 April and took charge of the military radiation-reconnaissance and decontamination effort. With the plant's own dosimeters dead or floored and its reported figures wildly low, the commission had no reliable picture of the fields around the open reactor — so Pikalov rode the reconnaissance vehicle right up to the wrecked Unit 4 himself, rather than order a young soldier to make the lethal run, and brought back the first authoritative readings: the graphite was burning, and the radiation was far above anything management had admitted. He absorbed about 1.37 Sv (reported at the time as ~150 roentgen) doing it — a dose that damaged his health for the rest of his life. He directed the chemical troops through the containment, was made Hero of the Soviet Union in December 1986, and died on 29 March 2003, aged 78. documented (The vivid touches often attached to the drive — ramming a closed gate, telling his young driver "stay here, son, you've still to become a father" — are reported/dramatized flourishes; the documented core is that the three-star commander of the Chemical Troops personally ran the recon and took a serious dose.)

The airdrops — heroic, but they mostly missed

From 27 April to ~2 May (the intensive phase; drops tapered on to ~10 May), Mi-8, Mi-6 and Mi-26 helicopters flew ~1,800 sorties over the open core and dumped ~5,000 tonnes into the crater — roughly 2,400 t lead, 1,800 t sand & clay, 800 t dolomite and 40 t boron carbide — crews hovering in fields of thousands of R/h, laying sheets of the dropped lead on their cabin floors for shielding, under a 25-roentgen dose limit. But the post-accident Kurchatov/Borovoi survey of the ruins found little trace of the dropped material where the fuel actually was — most of it missed, and the blanket may have trapped heat, feeding the temperature rise around 1–2 May that revived melt fears. The drops helped smother the graphite fire and the airborne release, but did not "seal" the core. reported / disputed

The helicopter crash — the real one was in October

On 2 October 1986, an Mi-8 (call sign "Cup-2") dropping polyvinyl-acetate decontaminant glue to trap radioactive dust passed over the reactor and its rotor blades struck the cable of a tall construction crane raised for the sarcophagus; the blades shattered and it fell just outside the reactor building, killing all four crew — Capt. Vladimir Vorobyov, Sr. Lt. Alexander Yungkind, Sr. Lt. Leonid Khristich, and Ensign Nikolai Ganzhuk. (A KGB report suggested the pilot had been blinded by low sun; this Capt. Vladimir Vorobyov is a different man from the plant's civil-defense chief Serafim Vorobyov.) It was caught on film — which is why it is so widely remembered, and why the 2019 series folds a crash into the April firefight (see Appendix C). The real crash came five months after the accident, during the sarcophagus build — not during the fire. documented

The cover-up, and the wider harm

The failure to tell people did not end at Pripyat's edge.

The May Day parade in Kyiv · 1 May 1986. Five days after the explosion, as the wind swung the plume toward Kyiv and the city's radiation climbed, the authorities let the annual May Day parade go ahead on the Khreshchatyk — hundreds of thousands in the open, children in short sleeves at the front, marching under the fallout to project calm and avert "panic." Ukrainian Party chief Volodymyr Shcherbytsky led it. documented / disputed
Whether Moscow ordered it over his objection, or he went ahead for fear of Moscow, is genuinely disputed — the parade under rising radiation is documented; the command chain is not.
Gorbachev's eighteen-day silence. The Soviet leader said nothing publicly until 14 May 1986, when Mikhail Gorbachev finally addressed the nation on television — calling the accident unprecedented, insisting "the worst is behind us," and devoting much of the speech to attacking Western reporting as "a mountain of lies." The delay itself became part of the scandal — a test glasnost plainly failed. documented
The phone lines cut. The clampdown was physical, too: the KGB cut Pripyat's telephone communications — the city's long-distance lines by daybreak on the 26th, the local lines dead by nightfall — to stop residents spreading word or calling out; a first KGB directive restricted access to international lines "to prevent the leak of information." documented
This deliberate severing of the town's lines is the real thing — not the plant's own Unit-4 control-room phone, which the explosion had already killed in the first minute (see Part I).
The milk, and the iodine that was not given. The children's thyroid cancers trace above all to one pathway: iodine-131 fell on the pasture, cows ate the grass, and children drank the fresh local milk (leafy-vegetable and inhalation routes were minor), with some individual thyroid doses reaching tens of grays. Stable-iodine tablets — which block the uptake if taken in time — were largely not distributed, and dairy not promptly banned, in the worst-hit Soviet areas. Next door, Poland gave stable iodine to ~10.5 million children and cut its thyroid dose sharply. In that sense the mass thyroid-cancer wave was preventable. documented
The wider evacuation, and the animals. The clearance ran far beyond Pripyat: the old town of Chernobyl (~12,000–14,000) and more than ninety villages were emptied — about 116,000 people within the 30 km zone by mid-May. Cleanup squads then went in to shoot the abandoned pets and livestock and bury radioactive topsoil, and in places whole villages. documented / reported
The free-roaming dogs of the zone today descend from the animals left behind.
The threat to Kyiv's water. The plant sits on the Pripyat River, feeding the Dnieper reservoir cascade — drinking water for millions, Kyiv's ~2.5 million among them — and the river was in spring flood. Fear that caesium and strontium, or a melt-through, would poison the supply drove a major but little-remembered engineering effort in early May: manipulating the reservoir gates, protecting the intakes, and standing up a Dnieper radiation-monitoring system that runs to this day. documented

The doctors, and the scientists who went into the ruin

Dr. Angelina Guskova (1924–2015) — who ran the medical response. By 1986 Guskova, a neurologist and radiation-medicine specialist who had treated radiation casualties since the Soviet bomb programme of the late 1940s, was the most experienced acute-radiation-syndrome clinician in the world, with well over a thousand cases behind her. As clinical head at Moscow's Hospital No. 6 she directed the triage and treatment of the Chernobyl casualties — the first ~129 flown in within about a day — and it was her Soviet ARS classification and protocols that framed their care. documented
Robert Gale, Armand Hammer, and the bone-marrow effort. Through the American industrialist Armand Hammer, who had a personal line to Gorbachev, the U.S. haematologist Dr. Robert Gale was brought to Hospital No. 6 — a rare moment of Cold-War medical cooperation. Gale assisted with the 13 bone-marrow transplants; the results were poor, most recipients dying, and the strategy is now judged largely ineffective here — the men were dying of skin and internal burns, not marrow failure alone. documented
Alexander Borovoi and the "Complex Expedition." From late 1986 the Kurchatov Institute's Chernobyl Complex Expedition, led scientifically by Alexander Borovoi with Konstantin Checherov, went into the ruined building — in coveralls and respirators, with little shielding, like the "stalkers" the zone later inspired — to find where the roughly 180-plus tonnes of nuclear fuel had gone. It was they who mapped the fuel-containing masses and, in late 1986, found the Elephant's Foot (Korneyev's famous photograph came a decade later, ~1996). documented / disputed
Checherov later argued far less fuel remained under Unit 4 than official estimates — a minority view the mainstream corium literature does not accept.

The valve-drain beneath the reactor · 6 May

The "Chernobyl divers" — Ananenko, Bespalov & Baranov

6 May 1986 — draining the bubbler pool under the ruined reactor

Water from the firefighting and the operators' own futile injection had pooled in the bubbler pool — the two-storey pressure-suppression reservoir directly beneath the reactor, holding on the order of 20,000 m³. The danger was the corium: the white-hot melt of fuel, cladding, graphite and concrete was burning downward through the biological shield, and scientists (Velikhov, Legasov, and Belarusian physicist Vasily Nesterenko) feared that if it reached the pooled water, the flash to steam would rupture what was left of the structure and hurl far more fuel and fission products into the air — a second release potentially worse than the first — while also threatening the still-running Units 1–3 and driving contamination into the groundwater and the Dnieper. (The "3–5 megaton" figure, from Nesterenko, is disputed: a steam blast is violent but not a multi-megaton nuclear yield — Appendix C.) The commission ordered the pool drained by hand.

All three survived. Boris Baranov d. 2005 (heart disease); Ananenko & Bespalov lived on. Decorated twice by Ukraine — Order "For Courage" (Poroshenko, 26 Apr 2018) and Hero of Ukraine (Zelensky, 27 Jun 2019), Baranov posthumously in each. Not electrician Anatoly Baranov (✝ 20 May 1986). In interviews decades on Ananenko stays matter-of-fact — he knew where the valves were, so he went — and gently corrects the "suicide squad" retellings: they expected to walk back out, and did. "I never felt like a hero. I was doing my job."

The cleanup, and the months after — the famous episodes

Beyond the ~24-hour window this account focuses on, the response ran on for years and produced its own famous stories — here in brief, dated, with the popular exaggerations corrected.

The miners · mid-May → late June 1986. Fearing the molten corium would melt through the foundation slab into the water table, the commission had coal miners tunnel in from beneath Unit 3 — a ~136 m gallery — to dig a chamber under Unit 4 for a cooling heat-exchanger. About 400 of them (388 — 154 Moscow/Tula, 234 Donbas) worked round-the-clock for ~six weeks in stifling underground heat (no forced ventilation, for fear of drawing in contamination), stripped to very little. The heat-exchanger was never needed — the corium solidified without breaching the slab. documented
The "a quarter of the miners died" line is the HBO caption, unsupported — none developed ARS (see Appendix C).
The robots that failed, and the "bio-robots" · Sept–Oct 1986. To clear radioactive graphite and fuel off the Unit-3 roof, dozens of remote machines were tried — the Soviet STR-1 (built on the Lunokhod moon-rover lineage) and a West German police robot (the MF-2, popularly the "Joker") — but fields of 10,000–15,000+ R/h killed their electronics in minutes. So 3,828 soldiers and reservists — the "bio-robots" — cleared it by hand in shifts of ~40–90 seconds, then were finished with radiation work for life under the 25-rem cap. Led by Maj.-Gen. Nikolai Tarakanov, who named the roof zones after women (Katya, Natasha and Masha — the worst, ~12,000 R/h). Afterward a red flag was raised on the ~78 m vent stack — and replaced more than once as the radiation shredded it. documented / reported
The sarcophagus · June → 30 Nov 1986. A concrete-and-steel "Shelter Object" was thrown up over Unit 4 in a ~206-day build — over 400,000 m³ of concrete, ~7,300 t of steel, some 90,000 workers, much of it placed remotely because no one could stand near the walls. A hasty, temporary structure, it decayed over the decades — which is why the New Safe Confinement arch was slid over it in 2016. documented
The Elephant's Foot · found Dec 1986. Deep under the reactor, searchers found a solidified flow of corium — the lava-like fuel melt — shaped like a wrinkled foot, reading about 10,000 R/h, fatal in roughly five minutes. The famous photo of a man beside it is inspector Artur Korneyev, taken ~1996; contrary to the legend that "everyone who photographed it died," Korneyev lived on for decades (interviewed in 2014). documented
The liquidators. Some ~600,000 people were granted "liquidator" status for the 1986–90 cleanup, under a 1986 emergency dose limit of 25 rem (250 mSv) (recorded average ~120 mSv). Two fates became well known: heavy-lift pilot Anatoly Grishchenko, who flew drops onto the reactor, later developed radiation-linked leukaemia, had a bone-marrow transplant in Seattle, and died in July 1990; and cameraman Vladimir Shevchenko, whose film of the reactor is speckled where radiation struck the emulsion directly, and who died of radiation illness on 30 March 1987. documented / reported
The years after — what the health record actually shows. Beyond the ~31 immediate deaths (Part V), the one clearly-measured population effect is a wave of thyroid cancer in people exposed as children — from iodine-131 in milk, which the missing stable-iodine prophylaxis (Part III) let into their thyroids. UNSCEAR counts ~6,000+ cases, but with very high survival (~15 deaths); the survivors carry a visible mark — the horizontal throat scar from thyroid surgery, common enough to earn the folk name the "Chernobyl necklace." Against that, the feared hereditary catastrophe did not materialise: 2021 NIH/NCI whole-genome studies found no excess new (heritable) mutations in children conceived after their parents' exposure — there was no "generation of mutants." The cruellest twist is that much of the human cost came from fear rather than dose: radiation panic drove an estimated 100,000–200,000 unnecessary abortions across Europe. Broader long-term excess-cancer projections are model-dependent (WHO/IAEA ~4,000 among the most-exposed groups, wider estimates into the tens of thousands); the honest figure is a range, not a headline (see Appendix C). documented

Part V — The dead

Two immediate deaths, and 28 from acute radiation syndrome

The internationally accepted acute toll is two immediate deaths and 28 deaths from acute radiation syndrome (ARS) within the first ~3–4 months, out of 134 confirmed ARS cases (of ~237 hospitalised, ~600 on site). Most died at Hospital No. 6 and are buried at Mitino Cemetery, Moscow, in sealed zinc coffins under concrete because their bodies were radioactive.

What ARS is, and how they got it. Acute radiation syndrome follows a very large whole-body dose absorbed in a short time: it wipes out the bone marrow and strips the gut lining, so death comes over days to weeks — usually after a deceptive "latent" period when the victim briefly seems to recover, then collapses. The men below did not die of a vague "radiation" — each took his dose in a specific place doing a specific thing: fighting the fire on the graphite-strewn roofs, opening valves by hand in radioactive water, looking into the open core, being scalded by radioactive steam, or standing guard in the open. The "how & where" is given for each.

What was released

What actually came out

The document traces the doses to people and where the fallout landed; this covers the thing in between — what and how much radioactivity actually escaped the reactor. Figures are from UNSCEAR (2000) and the OECD-NEA assessment, the standard source-term references.

It was not one burst but a ten-day plume. The release ran from the explosion on 26 April to about 6 May, in three phases: a large first-day release thrown out mechanically by the explosion and the burning graphite; a decline over 27 April–1 May as the fires were fought and the airdrops smothered the crater; then a second rise around 2–5 May as the covered, un-cooled fuel heated back up and drove volatiles out again — until it fell away sharply after ~6 May as the core finally cooled.

How much. The total is most often given as ~5,300 PBq (5.3 × 1018 becquerels) excluding the noble gases, or about 14 EBq all told — more than half of it biologically inert noble gas (mainly xenon-133). Only a few percent of the fuel by mass (~3.5%) escaped — but near-all of the volatile isotopes did, which is what mattered for health.

How it compares

Chernobyl is one of only two events ever rated Level 7 — the top of the International Nuclear Event Scale (INES) — the other being Fukushima Daiichi (2011). Chernobyl released several times more than Fukushima (whose atmospheric release was on the order of ~10–20% of Chernobyl's) and, unlike Fukushima, threw out fuel and graphite directly from an open, burning core with no containment. Three Mile Island (1979, INES Level 5) is the contrast that proves the point: its core partly melted, but the containment held and the release to the public was negligible.

The full story, start to finish

One continuous narrative — from the first foundations to the lasting scars

Everything above, told once more as a single unbroken thread — from the decision to build a power station on the Pripyat River, through the night the reactor tore itself apart and the people who lived and died in it, to the decades of consequence that followed. It repeats the parts rather than replacing them: the same facts, the same grading, nothing invented. Where the story reaches before and after the disaster core, the new material is graded like the rest — and, as flagged below, much of the fine-grained pre-1986 town detail rests on enthusiast local histories rather than primary archives, so it is offered as well-corroborated but a tier softer than the reactor physics and the casualty record.

Before the accident — building the plant and the town (1966–1983)

The station began as a line on a Soviet planning map. In 1966 a USSR Council of Ministers decree approved a national build-out of nuclear plants, one of them to feed the "Central" energy region of the Ukrainian SSR. Surveyors weighed sixteen candidate sites; the two finalists were near Ladyzhyn on the Southern Buh and a stretch of the Pripyat River's right bank near the village of Kopachi, about 15 km from the old town of Chornobyl and ~130 km north of Kyiv. In 1967 the Pripyat site — by the Yanov (Yaniv) railway station — was chosen: close to the Kyiv grid, with abundant river cooling water, rail access, and low-value farmland. [reported — from local histories and OECD-NEA's site description.] The plant was named the V. I. Lenin Nuclear Power Station, a Lenin statue at its gate.

To run it, Moscow appointed a young thermal-power engineer, Viktor Bryukhanov — born 1 December 1935 in Tashkent, an electrical engineer who had risen to deputy chief engineer at the Slavyansk thermal power plant. He came to the empty site in 1970, aged 34, as both construction chief and director — a capable builder, but not a reactor physicist, a fact that would matter.

Three kilometres from the reactors, a town rose with them. Pripyat was founded on 4 February 1970, named for the river, the ninth "atomgrad" in the USSR, granted city status in 1979. Moscow architects laid it out on a "triangle principle" of tower and flat-blocks — roughly five microdistricts, ~149 buildings and ~13,000 flats. Designed for perhaps 75,000–85,000, it held about 49,000 at the end (49,360, 49,400, or "~50,000" appear), and it was strikingly young — average age about 26, over a fifth under eighteen. Its chief city architect from 1979 was Maria Protsenko, who would draw the map for its evacuation. [population and youth documented; the design target and building counts rest on local histories — reported.]

Pripyat was a Soviet showcase: the Palace of Culture "Energetik," the "Lazur" swimming pool, the "Prometheus" cinema, around fifteen kindergartens for some 5,000 children and five secondary schools for ~6,700 pupils, the Hotel Polissya, a river port, ~25 shops and ~27 cafés, a stadium and football club, and — most poignantly — a new amusement park whose Ferris wheel was set to open on May Day, 1 May 1986, five days after the reactor blew. It never officially opened. Care ran through Medical-Sanitary Unit No. 126 (MSCh‑126), the ~410-bed hospital that would take the first casualties.

The reactors came online one by one. First concrete was poured on 15 August 1972. Unit 1 reached the grid on 26 September 1977; Unit 2 in December 1978; Unit 3 in 1981; and Unit 4 — the one that would fail — was commissioned in late December 1983. The four RBMK‑1000 units gave about 4,000 MWe (each ~3,200 MWt), cooled by a ~22 km² pond. Units 5 and 6 were under construction; Unit 5 was ~70% complete, targeted to start on 7 November 1986 — work the accident would halt and, by 1989, abandon.

The reactor that could not be allowed to fail — the RBMK and its hidden flaw

The RBMK‑1000 was a Soviet original: a graphite-moderated, water-cooled, channel-type reactor finalised in the mid-1960s under the scientific direction of Anatoly Aleksandrov (Kurchatov Institute) and chief designer Nikolai Dollezhal (NIKIET). It was favoured because it sidestepped the Soviet industry's inability to forge a giant pressure vessel — which also made it correspondingly cheaper to build; it ran on low-enriched (~2%) fuel, could be refuelled while running, and descended from plutonium-production reactors. Its core held on the order of 1,661 fuel channels.

It also carried two flaws that would kill. First, a large positive void coefficient: when the coolant boiled, the reaction sped up, making the reactor dangerously unstable below ~20–30% power. Second, and decisively, its control rods were tipped with graphite: dropping from fully withdrawn, the graphite ends first pushed absorbing water out of the bottom of the core, briefly adding reactivity — a positive spike at the very start of an emergency scram. Safe operation also depended on keeping enough rods in the core; the true floor was ~26–30 equivalent rods, but operators were taught to treat 15 as the limit.

None of this reached the men at the controls. That the RBMK could turn dangerously unstable was not unknown — at the Leningrad‑1 RBMK in 1975 a fuel-channel accident driven by a positive reactivity surge damaged around 32 fuel assemblies and released contamination over the Gulf of Finland, an early warning of the type's positive-feedback behaviour (the exact 1975 date is given variously across sources). And the specific graphite-tip "positive scram" flaw that would actually trigger the 1986 explosion was pinned down later still — around 1983, in RBMK commissioning tests — documented and circulated among senior designers, yet never passed to the reactor operators.

And the reactor type's fragility had been demonstrated at Chernobyl itself, four years before the disaster. On 9 September 1982, during a restart after a planned overhaul, as Unit 1 was brought back up toward power, a central fuel channel ruptured — blamed on operator error and disrupted coolant flow. The fuel assembly was destroyed and part of the core damaged (the detailed reconstructions that put it at ~20% power, ~3.5% of the core, and a partly-wetted graphite stack rest on a single account and are less firmly corroborated); a release of radioactive material spread over the plant's own grounds and into Pripyat, and the crews who cleaned it up took heavy doses. The reactor was out of action for about eight months. And, as with everything else, the accident was classified and kept from the public — so the town living beside the plant, and the very operators who would run the test in 1986, were never told that their own reactor had already melted a channel once.

Those reports stayed classified, and the design fixes were not made before 26 April 1986. The crews believed the emergency-shutdown button was a pure fail-safe. It was not.

The managers who would preside over the test embodied the plant's culture. Nikolai Fomin, the chief engineer (born 1937, an electrician by training), like Bryukhanov came from thermal power, not reactor physics. Anatoly Dyatlov, deputy chief engineer for operations — born 1931, a Moscow Engineering Physics Institute graduate who had assembled submarine reactors at Komsomolsk‑on‑Amur (and taken a serious dose in an accident there) before transferring to Chernobyl in 1973 — was the real reactor man of the three, strict and formidable. It was Dyatlov who would run the test.

The test that waited four years (25 April 1986)

The experiment was mundane on paper: if grid power failed, could a turbogenerator coasting on its own inertia keep the cooling pumps turning for the ~40–60 seconds until the diesels came up? It was an electrical test, run by a visiting specialist, Gennady Metlenko. The plant had tried and failed to complete it three times before — in 1982, 1984 and 1985 — and new voltage regulators prompted a fourth attempt during Unit 4's maintenance shutdown.

At 01:06 on 25 April the operators began lowering power from full; by 03:47 it sat near half. At 14:00 the Emergency Core Cooling System was isolated — and, almost at once, the Kyiv grid dispatcher asked that further reduction wait to cover the evening peak. So the reactor idled at ~1,600 MWt for roughly nine hours, long enough for the neutron poison xenon‑135 to build. The real cost was human: it pushed the test out of the prepared day shift's hands and into those of the night shift. At 23:10 the dispatcher released the unit and the reduction resumed.

Into the night — the reactor turns unstable (26 April, 00:00–01:23)

At midnight the shift changed. Aleksandr Akimov's night crew took the control room from Yuri Tregub's evening shift; Tregub stayed on, off the clock, to watch. At the panels were Leonid Toptunov (25, months in the senior seat) on the rods, Boris Stolyarchuk on unit systems, Igor Kirschenbaum on the turbine; Dyatlov directed. Elsewhere: Khodemchuk at the pumps; Kurguz and Genrikh in the central hall; Shashenok in room 604; Yuvchenko in his office at +12.5; Davletbayev with his turbine machinists; Lelechenko and Baranov in the electrical shop; Perevozchenko watching the rod indicators with trainees Proskuryakov and Kudryavtsev.

Almost at once it went wrong. Around 00:28, during a control transfer, power fell to about 30 MWt — near shutdown, the core now deeply xenon-poisoned. (This was long blamed on Toptunov; INSAG‑7 walked that back.) To claw power back the operators withdrew rods far beyond prudence. By 01:03 the reactor sat at only ~200 MWt — into the unstable zone — yet the test proceeded. The crew ran all eight pumps (01:07) and tripled feed-water flow (01:19); each step demanded still more rods out. By 01:22:30 the margin had sunk to about 6–8 equivalent rods, less than half the mandated minimum.

01:23:40 — the scram, and the explosions

At 01:23:04 the test began: Kirschenbaum closed the No. 8 turbine's stop valves, Metlenko called "Oscillograph — on!", and the coasting pumps started to slow — and the reactor did not trip here, because the automatic shutdown on loss of both turbines had been deliberately disabled earlier in the night so the test could be retried. Coolant flow fell, boiling rose, reactivity climbed. Thirty-six seconds later, at 01:23:40, Akimov ordered "AZ‑5!" and Toptunov pressed the shutdown button. The rods began to descend — and their graphite tips reached the bottom of the core first, spiking reactivity exactly where it must not. Power tore upward to something like a hundred times full power in about four seconds.

At roughly 01:23:45 the first explosion shattered the core region and heaved the ~1,000-tonne shield "Elena" off its seat. Valery Khodemchuk, at his pumps, was killed instantly — the first to die; his body was never recovered. Two or three seconds later a second, larger explosion ejected fuel and burning graphite, tore off the roof, and lit the graphite fire. Radioactive steam burst into the central hall, scalding Kurguz, who forced the door shut to shield others; Genrikh, less burned, kept his head and showered and changed before evacuating, cutting his dose enough to live; Shashenok, in room 604, was crushed and mortally hurt. (The exact second is a reconstruction — 01:23:45 is best-supported; seismic records show two shocks ~2.7 s apart.)

The first hour inside the ruin (01:24–02:00)

In his windowless room the walls had "bent like rubber"; Yuvchenko thought war had broken out. His phone to Control Room 4 was dead, but a call from Control Room 3 sent him for stretchers; he started down and was stopped by a blackened, bloodied figure — his friend Degtyarenko, known only by his voice. A scalded operator begged him, "Help Khodemchuk — he's in the pump room," but the pump side had collapsed and was impassable. In the control room, Dyatlov sent the trainees to lower the rods by hand and Perevozchenko to look for Khodemchuk.

Then came the sequence that decided who lived. In the corridor Yuvchenko met Tregub, sent to open the emergency-coolant valves; the two went down two flights into knee-deep water to a jammed door — the tanks "torn apart like wet cardboard" — then out into the open, where they saw a pillar of ionised blue light rising from the open core. Running back, they were stopped by Perevozchenko and the two trainees; Yuvchenko warned them it was senseless — the reactor was gone — but Perevozchenko insisted they see it from above. Tregub carried the word to the control room; Yuvchenko, with the only torch, climbed with the other three to the reactor-hall airlock, where he braced the multi-tonne door with his left shoulder while the three looked into the glowing core — all three fatally dosed. When Yuvchenko moved to look, Perevozchenko shoved him back and the door slammed: "There's nothing to see here." That shove, and the shielding, almost certainly saved his life. He was flown to Moscow that evening and spent about a year in Hospital No. 6, kept alive by constant transfusions — "I lived on other people's blood" — one of almost the only reactor-building men of that night to live; married, with a small son, Kirill, he died of leukaemia in 2008, aged 47.

And here the tragedy of the night set: the reports changed nothing. Tregub told Akimov and Dyatlov what they had seen; Dyatlov held that a feed-water tank had burst and the reactor was whole. The trainees reported the core open — and were disbelieved. Perevozchenko carried the same word and met the same refusal. Man after man reported the truth and was sent back into the radiation — the trainees to lower non-existent rods, Tregub and Yuvchenko to the valves, Perevozchenko on alone through >10,000 R/hr rubble toward Khodemchuk, already dead and entombed — while the water kept pouring into a reactor that no longer existed. In the turbine hall Davletbayev, Brazhnik, Akimov and Palamarchuk fought oil and hydrogen fires that could have reached Unit 3; in the electrical shop Lelechenko waded three times through radioactive water to cut the hydrogen, sparing his younger men. Palamarchuk and dosimetrist Gorbachenko carried the dying Shashenok out of room 604. Out on the wrecked exterior, amid scattered graphite, Tregub said to Dyatlov, "This is Hiroshima" — and Dyatlov, still unable to accept the core was gone, said nothing.

The firefighters on the roof

The alarm went out within minutes. Lt. Vladimir Pravik's plant brigade reached the north side by ~01:28; he raised the highest alarm, mobilising the whole oblast. Lt. Viktor Kibenok's Pripyat crew — Vasily Ignatenko, Vashchuk, Tytenok, Tishura — followed, and Major Leonid Telyatnikov took overall command ~01:40. They came in ordinary gear, no respirators, no working dosimeters, and no idea the debris was intensely radioactive; some handled chunks of graphite bare-handed. They kept the fire from Unit 3 and won: the last surface fires were out by ~06:35, the response peaking near 37 crews, ~186 firefighters and ~81 engines. Dr. Valentin Belokon recognised radiation sickness, not burns, and worked the casualties until he himself began vomiting. Telyatnikov survived — made Hero of the Soviet Union, he died of cancer in 2004 — but the roof men had taken the doses that would kill them within weeks. Kibenok told the ambulance doctor there were no burn casualties, then dropped to his knees vomiting at his feet; in hospital, in the deceptive calm before the collapse, Pravik wrote his wife Nadezhda an optimistic letter, sure he would recover. Vasily Ignatenko, so radioactive he would be buried in a soldered zinc coffin set in concrete, was the husband of Lyudmila Ignatenko, whose account of watching him disintegrate — "every day I met a brand-new person" — became, through Alexievich, the single most-cited human testimony of the disaster.

The long denial — dawn and the day shift (06:00–20:00)

Through the pre-dawn hours the leadership relayed reassurance built on a false premise. Bryukhanov reached the bunker ~02:30 and told Moscow the situation was under control. The only working dosimeters read to ~3.6–4 R/h — the high-range meter buried, another failed — so that ceiling went up the chain while true fields ran to thousands. Civil-defense chief Serafim Vorobyov, using the one instrument that could read further — a military DP‑5 (200 R/h ceiling) — found the needle slammed off its scale and urged the town be warned; Bryukhanov stalled and kept it out of the reports. From ~06:00, Akimov and Toptunov, though relieved, waded back into the flooded gallery and cranked the valves by hand for about half an hour — futile, the lines severed, fatal for both. Akimov, dying in Moscow weeks later, swollen and turned deep reddish-brown, would insist to the end that the crew had done nothing wrong — "we did everything correctly… Why?" — a cry INSAG‑7 would later half-vindicate. Shashenok died about 06:00 — the second death.

As the day shift arrived and took in the graphite strewn on the ground, two engineers, Orlov and Smagin, traded the grim joke that fixed the moment the truth became undeniable — "Did these graphite blocks lie here before the accident?" "Not really; we'd cleaned up for the 1st of May." Meanwhile Pripyat woke to a warm, sunny Saturday, unwarned: children at open-air school exercises, anglers at the warm outflow (among them Pustovoit and Protasov, who took very high doses), several weddings, the amusement park being readied. As invisible fallout settled through the day, some residents gathered on balconies to watch the coloured glow over the plant — one, Alexievich records, lifting his children to see it, standing in black dust. Around 09:00, on Fomin's order, deputy chief engineer Anatoly Sitnikov — a reactor physicist whose word could not be waved away — climbed to a high vantage and looked down into the open crater, confirming the core destroyed. He took a massive dose (some accounts ~15 Sv to the head), reported the truth — and was still only partly believed; the order to pump water stood. He died on 30 May. No stable-iodine prophylaxis was given — the omission that seeded the later thyroid-cancer wave.

The Commission, and the evacuation (evening 26 Apr → 27 Apr)

The State Commission reached Pripyat about 20:00: chairman Boris Shcherbina, chief scientist Valery Legasov, Energy Minister Maiorets, the designers' deputy minister Meshkov and others; Legasov saw a crimson glow over half the sky on the approach. That night the leadership finally accepted the reactor was destroyed; after the dosimetry prognosis, Shcherbina decided to evacuate the next day and telephoned Premier Ryzhkov — "Pripyat has to be evacuated… people are living it up full blast, weddings are going on" — who answered "evacuation tomorrow." The worst-exposed were flown that night to Moscow's Hospital No. 6 under Dr. Angelina Guskova, while Pripyat slept a second night in the contaminated town.

At ~13:10 on 27 April a radio announcement told residents to prepare to leave "for three days." At 14:00 it began — about 1,200 buses cleared ~47,000 people in roughly two-and-a-half to three-and-a-half hours, calm and orderly, from Maria Protsenko's maps. Told they would return in three days, most left pets and belongings. They never came back.

The world finds out — Forsmark, and the silence broken (28 April)

Moscow said nothing publicly. On the morning of 28 April, workers arriving at the Forsmark plant in Sweden — about 1,100 km away — set off contamination alarms, and when the fallout was traced back the USSR was forced to speak. That evening a terse ~20-second TASS bulletin — "An accident has occurred at the Chernobyl power station…" — became the first public admission, about two and a half days after the blast. The ~36-hour blackout for Pripyat's residents and this delayed disclosure are two different failures, often blurred into the myth that it was "hidden for weeks" (Appendix C).

And even then the priority was appearances over safety. On 1 May, as the plume swung toward Kyiv and the city's radiation rose, the authorities let the annual May Day parade go ahead — hundreds of thousands in the open, children at the front — to project calm; Ukrainian Party chief Volodymyr Shcherbytsky led it (whether ordered from Moscow or done for fear of it is disputed). And Gorbachev himself did not speak until 14 May, eighteen days on, when he finally went on television to call the accident unprecedented, say "the worst is behind us," and spend much of the speech attacking Western coverage as "a mountain of lies." (Part IV carries both in full.)

The battle to contain it — airdrops, divers, miners, robots, the sarcophagus (May–November 1986)

From 27 April to ~2 May, helicopters flew perhaps 1,800 sorties and dropped about 5,000 tonnes of sand, clay, boron, dolomite and lead, crews hovering in fields of thousands of R/h under Col. Nikolai Antoshkin. It was heroic and largely missed — little reached the fuel, and the blanket may have trapped heat. The first authoritative radiation picture came not from the plant but from Col.-Gen. Vladimir Pikalov, the commander of the Chemical Troops and a decorated veteran of Stalingrad and Kursk, who reached the site on 26 April and drove the reconnaissance vehicle right up to the wrecked unit himself rather than send a young soldier to make the lethal run, bringing back the first honest readings — the graphite burning, the fields far above the plant's figures — and taking about 1.37 Sv doing it. He was made Hero of the Soviet Union, and died in 2003.

As the corium burned downward, Velikhov and Legasov feared it would reach the water in the bubbler pool and flash it to steam. On 6 May, three plant workers — Alexei Ananenko, who knew the valves, with Valeri Bespalov and shift supervisor Boris Baranov on the lamp — waded in and opened the two gate valves, their searchlight working, and heard the water rush out. They were inside about fifteen minutes and all three survived (the "divers died" story is false). Through mid-May to late June, about 400 coal miners tunnelled beneath Unit 3 to dig a chamber for a cooling heat-exchanger that was never needed (no miner developed ARS). In September–October, remote machines failed in the fields on the roof, so 3,828 soldiers, the "bio-robots," cleared it by hand in shifts of 40–90 seconds. And from June to 30 November 1986, a concrete-and-steel sarcophagus was thrown up over the ruin in ~206 days — a hasty, temporary tomb that would have to be re-covered decades later. That December, deep in a corridor beneath the reactor, searchers found the "Elephant's Foot" — a solidified flow of corium reading about 10,000 R/h, fatal in roughly five minutes; the inspector who later photographed it, Artur Korneyev, lived on for decades (Appendix C).

The dead, and the doses

The internationally accepted acute toll is two immediate deaths and 28 from acute radiation syndrome within about three months, out of 134 confirmed ARS cases. Each took his dose in a specific place doing a specific thing: Khodemchuk at his pumps, Shashenok at his gauges, the firefighters on the roof, Akimov and Toptunov back in the water, Sitnikov and the trainees looking into the core, Perevozchenko searching for Khodemchuk, Degtyarenko scalded at his pumps (the bloodied man Yuvchenko had met on the stairs), the guards Ivanenko and Luzganova standing their posts in the open. Because almost no one wore a dosimeter that could read the true fields, the individual figures are clinical reconstructions; the one complete picture is the dose-band distribution of the 134 cases (Grade IV, 6.5–16 Sv, 21 cases, 20 dead, down to Grade I, 41 cases, none dead). Most who died despite Hospital No. 6's care succumbed not to marrow failure but to skin/beta burns, pneumonitis and organ damage.

The reckoning — the trial, and Legasov (1987–1988)

Blame came the next summer. From 7 to 30 July 1987, in the House of Culture in the town of Chernobyl, six men stood trial. Bryukhanov, Fomin and Dyatlov were each sentenced to ten years; shift director Rogozhkin got five, Unit-4 chief Kovalenko three, inspector Laushkin two — believed the first criminal convictions of nuclear-plant officials anywhere. The verdict fixed blame on the operators; INSAG‑7 would later move much of it onto the concealed design flaws. Dyatlov, freed early on health grounds, wrote a memoir indicting the RBMK and died in Kyiv on 13 December 1995; Bryukhanov served about five years and died in Kyiv on 13 October 2021, aged 85; Fomin was released and reportedly returned to nuclear work at the Kalinin plant.

The scientist who became the disaster's public face met a darker end. Valery Legasov had led the Soviet delegation to the IAEA in Vienna in August 1986, where — to preserve the programme — he stressed operator error and played down the design flaws. He dictated five cassette tapes on the suppressed causes, and on 27 April 1988 — the day after the second anniversary — he hanged himself. In 1996 President Yeltsin named him a Hero of the Russian Federation, posthumously. The courtroom speech the 2019 series gives him is invented (Appendix C).

The plant that kept running, and its long closing (1986–2000)

Strange as it sounds now, Chernobyl kept generating for fourteen more years. Units 1, 2 and 3 were decontaminated and restarted. Unit 2 was shut for good after a turbine-hall fire on 11 October 1991; Unit 1 closed in November 1996. Western governments pressed for closure: a memorandum among the G7, the European Commission and Ukraine, signed in Ottawa on 20 December 1995, committed Ukraine to shut the plant by 2000 for aid. So on 15 December 2000, President Leonid Kuchma gave the order by television link and Unit 3 — the last — was powered down, closing the station. Decommissioning is a century-long project: the spent fuel is moving into a new Holtec dry store, ISF‑2 (built for 21,217 RBMK assemblies), with final demolition not planned until around 2064.

Sealing the wound again — the New Safe Confinement (1997–2019)

The 1986 sarcophagus was only ever meant to last decades, and it was failing. An international effort — the Shelter Implementation Plan (1997) and an EBRD-managed Shelter Fund (over €1.6 billion from 45 donors) — financed a permanent solution. Built by the French-led Novarka consortium, the New Safe Confinement is a steel arch 257 m across, 108 m high and about 36,000 tonnes — the largest movable land structure ever built. Assembled to one side to keep workers out of the radiation, it was slid over the reactor across 14–29 November 2016 and handed to Ukraine in July 2019, designed to stand about a hundred years. (Roughly €1.5 billion for the arch, ~€2.1 billion for the whole programme.)

The health legacy — what four decades actually show

Forty years on (the anniversary fell in 2026), the measurable record is narrower, and stranger, than either telling. The confirmed death toll is ~31. The one clear population signal is thyroid cancer in those who were children in 1986 — over 6,000 cases from iodine‑131 in contaminated cow's milk, but with very high survival (~15 deaths by 2008), the toll the missing iodine prophylaxis let happen (neighbouring Poland gave stable iodine to ~10.5 million children and largely spared them; the survivors' throat-surgery scar is nicknamed the "Chernobyl necklace"). The feared genetic catastrophe did not appear: in 2021, NIH/NCI whole-genome studies found no excess heritable mutations in children conceived after their parents' exposure. Among the ~600,000 liquidators — soldiers, reservists, miners, pilots and volunteers, not the doomed convicts of legend — no clear leukaemia excess was proven at first, with some later evidence among higher-dose workers and a definite rise in cataracts; two of their fates became well known, helicopter pilot Anatoly Grishchenko, who flew the drops and died in 1990 of radiation-linked leukaemia after a bone-marrow transplant in Seattle, and cameraman Vladimir Shevchenko, whose film of the reactor is speckled where the radiation struck the emulsion and who died in 1987. And the Chernobyl Forum's own conclusion was that the largest lasting harm was psychological and socioeconomic — anxiety, fatalism, stigma and shattered livelihoods among the displaced.

The zone — an accidental wilderness

The land bears the deepest mark. A 10 km then 30 km Exclusion Zone was sealed off; about 116,000 people were evacuated in 1986 and another ~220,000 resettled later, some 335,000 lives uprooted in all. The squads that emptied the villages also shot the abandoned pets and livestock and buried radioactive soil — the free-roaming dogs of the zone today descend from the animals left behind. Downwind, ~4 km² of pine turned ginger-red and died — the "Red Forest," still among the most radioactive open ground on Earth. The long-lived isotopes are caesium‑137 and strontium‑90 (~30-year half-lives). The fallout reached far: Belarus took the heaviest share (~60–70% of the Soviet deposition, ~23% of its territory), and radiocaesium was measurable across Scandinavia, Germany and Britain — where restrictions on Welsh and Cumbrian sheep farms were not fully lifted until 1 June 2012, and in Lapland the fallout settled into the lichen and then the reindeer, disrupting the Sami herders for years.

Yet with the people gone, wildlife returned in force — wolves, lynx, brown bears, European bison, elk and wild boar, with Przewalski's horses reintroduced in 1998 now over 150, and a few hundred elderly "self-settlers" (samosely) who went home and stayed. Scientists disagree on the fine print — Møller and Mousseau report harm in the hottest patches; census studies (Deryabina et al., 2015) find abundant, stable mammal populations — but the headline is settled: neither a lifeless wasteland nor a pristine paradise, and removing humans has helped the animals more than the radiation has hurt them (Appendix C).

Two landmarks bracket the zone's afterlife. To rehouse the plant's workers the USSR built an entirely new city, Slavutych, about 50 km east — begun in 1986 and the last city the Soviet Union ever founded. And rusting inside the zone still stands the Duga over-the-horizon radar ("Chernobyl‑2"), a ~150 m Cold-War early-warning antenna whose pulses were heard around the world in the 1970s–80s as the "Russian Woodpecker" — a reminder that this was a militarily secret place long before it became a byword for disaster.

The wider fallout — Europe, glasnost, and the end of an empire

Chernobyl's largest consequence may have been political. The clumsy secrecy — the town unwarned, the world learning from Sweden — collided with Gorbachev's glasnost and helped force it into reality; Gorbachev himself wrote in 2006 that the disaster, "even more than my launch of perestroika, was perhaps the real cause of the collapse of the Soviet Union." Out of it came a literature of witness — Svetlana Alexievich's Voices from Chernobyl (1997), which helped win her the 2015 Nobel Prize — and, decades on, a strange second life as a place people visit: regulated tours from around 2011, and a tourism surge after the 2019 HBO miniseries, with the zone later put forward for UNESCO recognition.

War comes to the ruins — 2022, and a strike in 2025

The story was not over. On 24 February 2022, the first day of Russia's full-scale invasion, Russian forces seized the Chernobyl plant, advancing through the zone from Belarus; disturbed topsoil briefly raised local readings, though the IAEA judged them within the operational range. Ukrainian staff were held on-site for roughly five weeks, one group of about fifty working over 600 hours straight; on 9 March the site lost external power and the IAEA lost its remote monitoring data. Ukrainian officials reported that Russian troops dug trenches in the contaminated Red Forest and that some fell ill — though the IAEA could not confirm the radiation-sickness claims. Russia withdrew around 31 March 2022 and Ukraine regained control. Then, on 14 February 2025, a drone (Ukraine blames Russia; Moscow denies) struck the New Safe Confinement, starting a fire that smouldered for weeks; the IAEA said the great arch had "lost its primary safety functions, including confinement capability," even as radiation held normal. The wound built to be sealed for a century had, within a decade, been torn at by war.

What Chernobyl left behind

Set end to end, the story is not the one popular memory keeps. It was not the recklessness of a few operators but a flawed reactor whose fatal secret was kept from the men who ran it; not a fire that sand from the sky put out, but a graphite core that burned for days and was smothered mostly by luck and by men who walked into fields that killed them; not "the divers who died" or "the miners, a quarter of them dead," but people who mostly lived, having done a plain job in an impossible place. The dead are countable and named — a pump operator entombed where he stood, an adjuster crushed at his gauges, the firefighters on the roof, the operators back in the water, the engineer who looked into the core, the guards who never left their posts. The living carried it in their bodies and their memories. And the land keeps the longest record of all: a sealed circle of forest and empty towns, quietly turning back into wilderness, that will outlast everyone who remembers why it was closed.

Appendix B — Known disagreements

Where the record itself is uncertain

Kept explicit so the account is honest rather than falsely precise.

  • Exact explosion time — best-supported 01:23:45 for the first blast, second ~01:23:47–48; other sources give :44/:47/:48. All are reconstructions.
  • Why AZ-5 was pressed — unresolved (INSAG-7). Press commonly cited 01:23:40; some DREG-based reconstructions ~01:23:39.
  • Cause of the ~00:28 power drop — INSAG-7 retracted the "operator error" attribution; cause unknown.
  • Akimov's death — 10 May (majority) vs 11 May (Higginbotham).
  • Sitnikov's death — 30 May (majority) vs 31 May.
  • Ignatenko's death — 13 May (better-supported) vs a minority 14 May (not to be confused with Vashchuk, 14 May).
  • The "tail" dead (Vershinin, Novik, Ivan Orlov, Shapovalov, Konoval) — now dated from Soviet-era liquidator / memorial records (Ivan Orlov 13 May, Shapovalov 19 May, Konoval 28 May, Vershinin 21 July, Novik 26 July), consistent across those compilations. As the IAEA publishes the count but no official name list, these rest a tier below the IAEA-corroborated core. Note two men named Orlov: Ivan Orlov died 13 May; Viacheslav Orlov, the called-in valve engineer, survived.
  • Bryukhanov's arrival — "not yet 2 a.m." vs ~02:30 — rendered here as ~02:30.
  • Commission arrival — ~20:00 (common) vs 20:20 (Legasov's precise figure).
  • The 01:22:30 "printout" — whether the crew actually received a live reading is disputed.

Appendix C — Myths kept out

Famous, but not historical

These appear in the popular imagination largely because of the 2019 HBO miniseries. They are excluded from the factual narrative above.

Dramatization

"3.6 roentgen — not great, not terrible."

A scripted line (spoken by the Dyatlov character). No source records anyone saying it. The real kernel: 3.6 R/h was the ceiling of the small dosimeters on hand; the true field was ~15,000–20,000+ R/h, and management genuinely under-read and downplayed it.

Undocumented legend

The "Bridge of Death."

The story that Pripyat residents gathered on a railway bridge to watch the fire and all later died is undocumented. Some residents did glimpse the glow (mostly from windows); at least one child witness survived. The "none survived" framing is dramatization.

Medically false

"The baby absorbed the radiation."

Emotionally faithful to Lyudmila Ignatenko's real testimony, but medically false — an externally irradiated person is not a radioactive source to others.

Compressed for drama

The instant graphite hand-burn.

The on-contact hand burn shown for Vasily Ignatenko is dramatized; beta burns develop over hours. But the underlying fact — firefighters handling graphite in ignorance — is documented.

Screenwriter's line

Legasov's "What is the cost of lies?"

Not from his tapes — though his disillusionment and 1988 suicide are real. The composite scientist "Ulana Khomyuk" and the helicopter crash during the initial fire are also inventions. The real Mi-8 crash was 2 October 1986 — a crane-cable strike during the sarcophagus build that killed all four crew, documented as real in Part IV; it came five months after the fire, not during it.

Undocumented legend

Pravik's eyes "turned from brown to blue."

Widely repeated of firefighter Vladimir Pravik, but no primary source records it — a romantic legend, not medicine.

Refuted embellishment

Perevozchenko watching the fuel-channel caps "dance."

Grigori Medvedev's scene of Perevozchenko in the central hall seeing the 350 kg channel caps jumping before the blast is called the foremost of his "fables" — Yuvchenko wrote that he could not have been there and never told the story. The phenomenon is plausible — as the fuel channels overpressured and ruptured, their caps could have been heaved and rattled in their sockets in a ripple across the reactor face — but if it happened it was essentially the same instant as the blast, not a scene anyone could stand and calmly watch and survive. It is the eyewitness that is disputed, not the physics.

Misattributed

"We didn't know death could be so beautiful."

A real Alexievich line — but from Nadezhda Vygovskaya, a Pripyat resident watching from town, not from the firefighters on the roof.

False

"The divers died days after draining the pool."

The three who opened the bubbler-pool valves on 6 May — Ananenko, Bespalov and Baranov — all survived; Baranov lived to 2005, the other two for decades. The "they died within days" claim is a persistent myth.

Unsupported caption

"A quarter of the miners died / 100 died before 40."

This is the HBO series' closing caption ("it is said…"), not a measured statistic — no study supports it, and none of the ~400 miners is recorded as having developed ARS. Their work was brutal and dangerous, but the "one-in-four died young" figure is unverified folklore. Their cooling heat-exchanger was, in the end, never even needed (Part IV).

False

"Everyone who photographed the Elephant's Foot died."

Inspector Artur Korneyev, who spent more time near the corium mass than almost anyone and took the famous photo, lived on for decades — interviewed in person in 2014.

Folklore

Three-eyed fish and giant mutant animals.

Real sub-lethal effects exist (cataracts, some tumours and sterility, localised wildlife changes), but there are no monster fauna. The viral "giant catfish" in the cooling pond are ordinary, old, well-fed wels catfish, not mutants; the zone is now, in effect, a wildlife refuge.

Disputed

"A second blast would have been 3–5 megatons and wrecked half of Europe."

The megaton figure traces to Belarusian physicist Vassili (Vasily) Nesterenko and is disputed. A steam explosion (or renewed core damage) was a genuine fear — the reason the bubbler pool was drained — but a multi-megaton nuclear yield was not physically on the table. (Nesterenko himself is real and important beyond the myth: he overflew the reactor, pressed — largely unheeded — for iodine and wider evacuation, was pushed out and harassed by the KGB, and in 1990 founded the independent BELRAD institute to monitor radiation in Belarusian children.)

Folklore, not prophecy

"Chernobyl was the 'Wormwood' foretold in Revelation."

A real cultural phenomenon, not a prophecy. Chornobyl is the Ukrainian name for a local wormwood/mugwort (Artemisia), and Revelation 8:10–11 names a star, "Wormwood," that falls and poisons the waters — a coincidence that fuelled a genuine rumour wave in 1986 (it reportedly reached President Reagan). It is recorded here as folklore that spread, explicitly not as literal prophecy — and the linguistics are loose (the generic Ukrainian word for wormwood is polyn; chornobyl is one specific variety).

Dramatized composite

Dyatlov: "You didn't see graphite, because it's not there!"

The screamed denial is a dramatized composite. The denial itself is real — plant management refused to accept the core had exploded and kept reporting the reactor intact even with graphite on the ground (graphite outside the core is unambiguous proof of its destruction). But the specific shouted line, and the framing of Dyatlov as a lone cartoon villain, are inventions — the series concentrates a whole institution's denial into one man. INSAG-7 (1992) later shifted the blame heavily off the operators.

Compressed for drama

The dosimeter scene — Sitnikov, the safe, and the "200-roentgen" meter.

The Episode-1 beat in which Sitnikov produces dosimeters — a high-range one that burns out the moment it is switched on, then another that pegs at 200 roentgen, all waved away by Bryukhanov and Fomin as faulty equipment — is a compression of two real threads. (1) The small dosimeters on hand pegged at their low ceilings, and the one higher-range military instrument that could read further — the DP-5 (200 R/h ceiling) — was itself pegged; that reading belonged chiefly to civil-defense chief Serafim Vorobyov, whose ~200 R/h figure management stalled on and kept out of the reports. (2) Sitnikov's actual documented role was the rooftop visual confirmation of the open core, which was disbelieved. The show fuses Vorobyov's dismissed reading and Sitnikov's disbelieved report into one Sitnikov-with-dosimeters scene. The specific "Sitnikov unlocks a safe to reach a better dosimeter" beat is not corroborated in the record, and the later "marched onto the roof at gunpoint" image is an HBO invention — Sitnikov went up as the senior physicist whose word could not be waved away, not under armed guard.

Oversimplified · reversed by INSAG-7

"It was operator error — the operators were reckless idiots."

The 1986 Soviet account (and INSAG-1) blamed the operators. The 1992 INSAG-7 revision moved the major blame onto the RBMK design: the large positive void coefficient and — decisively — the graphite-tipped control rods, whose insertion on a full AZ-5 scram briefly increased reactivity before shutting the reactor down (the "positive scram effect"). This flaw was known from a 1975 Leningrad RBMK incident but was concealed from the operators, who believed AZ-5 was a pure emergency stop. The crews did break procedures — but they were set up to fail by defects hidden from them.

Oversimplified

"They ran a routine test and switched off all the safety systems."

It was a specific turbine-coastdown (rundown) test. Power fell far below the target — partly from xenon-135 poisoning — and to recover it the operators withdrew too many rods, leaving a dangerously low reactivity margin; the AZ-5 scram into that unstable, high-void state triggered the rod-tip power spike. Certain trips were blocked to run the test (the turbine-trip scram, some ECCS interlocks), but "turned off all the safety systems" is a caricature — the fatal ingredient was the hidden rod-design flaw meeting an unstable configuration.

Real, but not the fix

"The helicopters dropped sand into the open core and smothered it."

The drops absolutely happened — from 27 April, Mi-6/Mi-8/Mi-26 crews flew ~1,800 sorties and dumped ~5,000 tons of sand, clay, boron, dolomite and lead from hover, at genuine risk in fields of thousands of R/h; it was one of the bravest efforts of the whole response. What's mythic is only the outcome: with no bombsights and only a small opening under the tilted lid to aim at, post-hoc surveys of the ruins found almost none of it reached the core — most of the load missed. It may have helped smother the graphite fire and cut the airborne release, but the melted core cooled largely on its own; the airdrops were not what sealed or "put out" the reactor.

Misleading framing

"The liquidators were prisoners / conscript suicide squads."

The ~600,000–700,000 liquidators were conscripted army reservists plus professionals and genuine volunteers — not prisoners, and not men knowingly sent to die. Conditions were harsh and safety information poor, but the "doomed convict squad" image is exaggerated (the three bubbler-pool "divers," for instance, all survived the mission). Long-term health effects are debated; a mass immediate death of liquidators did not happen.

Conflated timelines

"The USSR hid the disaster for weeks before evacuating."

Pripyat (~49,000 people) was evacuated ~36 hours after the blast, on the afternoon of 27 April — not weeks. What did drag on was disclosure: the KGB restricted communications, and Moscow only admitted the accident on 28 April, after Swedish monitoring stations detected the plume. A ~36-hour information blackout for residents is real; "hidden for weeks before the town was evacuated" is not.

Both extremes mislead

"Only 31 died" vs. "millions died."

The confirmed immediate toll is ~31 (2 killed at the scene, ~28 from acute radiation syndrome, 1 heart attack). Projected long-term excess cancer deaths are model-dependent: WHO/IAEA ~4,000 among the most-exposed groups, with wider estimates into the tens of thousands; the ~90,000+ Greenpeace-type figures are contested, and "millions" is unsupported. The honest answer is a bounded but uncertain range, not a single headline number.

False

"Vodka (or red wine) protects against radiation."

A widespread 1986 belief with no basis; alcohol does not shield against ionizing radiation and can worsen outcomes. The one genuinely useful chemical countermeasure was potassium iodide, to block radioactive iodine-131 from the thyroid — not vodka.

Invented scene

"Legasov exposed the design flaw at the operators' trial."

Legasov was not at the trial; the courtroom reveal is fictional (creator Craig Mazin calls it "inspired by factual circumstances"). Legasov did deliver a long report at the IAEA meeting in Vienna in August 1986 — but there he stressed operator error and downplayed the design flaws. His later private notes and his 1988 suicide (on the second anniversary) fed the "truth-teller" narrative the show compresses into a trial speech.

Contaminated yes · a source no

"Radiation is contagious — being near a dying patient poisons you."

Being struck by gamma or beta radiation does not turn a person's body into a radioactive source — so once a patient is decontaminated (clothing stripped, washed down), they are not dangerous to be near, and ARS is not transmissible. The HBO premise that dying Vasily Ignatenko lethally irradiated his wife and unborn child by proximity is medically wrong. But the responders genuinely carried radioactivity: their clothing, skin and hair were coated in radioactive dust (fuel, fission products, graphite), and some had inhaled or swallowed particles — and that contamination is real and transferable until removed. It is why the firefighters' uniforms still lie in the Pripyat hospital basement, hazardous to this day, and why the most contaminated bodies (Ignatenko) were sealed in soldered zinc coffins under concrete. So a washed patient was not a walking reactor — but the fear-driven version did real harm: an estimated 100,000–200,000 unnecessary abortions across Europe after 1986.

Not borne out

"Chernobyl bred a generation of mutant children."

The real, documented effect is a wave of thyroid cancer from iodine-131 in milk — UNSCEAR counts ~6,000+ cases among those exposed as children/adolescents, but with very high survival (~15 deaths). By contrast, 2021 NIH/NCI whole-genome studies found no excess new (heritable) mutations in children conceived after their parents' exposure — directly contradicting the "mutant next generation" fear.

Oversimplified

"The zone will be uninhabitable for 20,000 years."

The ~20,000-year figure applies to the most contaminated ground at the reactor site, not the whole Exclusion Zone. Contamination is very uneven; long-lived caesium-137 and strontium-90 (~30-year half-lives) dominate the near-term dose, and parts of the zone are already lightly inhabited by returnee "self-settlers." A blanket "20,000 years for the whole area" is misleading.

Overstated

"The reactor could still explode again" (the 2021 neutron rise).

Sensors did detect rising neutron counts in a sub-reactor room (305/2) in 2021, showing slow fission continuing in the fuel-containing masses — but the material sits well below the threshold for a runaway chain reaction, and the New Safe Confinement would contain any small event. A real phenomenon with an alarmist conclusion, not a second 1986 in waiting.
